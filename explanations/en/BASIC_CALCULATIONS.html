The basic calculations stage: fitpipeline

Date: 30 September 2026 | Status: ✓ done

Overview

This stage takes a segment (a group of triangles from the mesh) and computes two things:

  1. Normalization and weighting: moves the vertices and the surface size into a standard coordinate frame, so the maths does not depend on units or position
  2. Initial hypotheses: guesses the primitive type (plane, cylinder, cone, sphere, torus) and its parameters in closed form

The modules created

1. geometry.py: normalization and sample sets

Separates the "normalization" (scaling and translation) from the geometry itself.

The Normalization class

Stores two parameters:

  • center: the centre of the segment in the original coordinate frame
  • scale: the representative length of the segment (in the original frame)

Two functions:

  • points_to_normalized(points): moves points from the original frame to the normalized one: (p − center) / scale
  • points_from_normalized(points): back to the original: p × scale + center

Why? If you fit in the normalized frame (uniform scale, centred), the parameters you get do not depend on where the mesh sits or how big it is. They map back exactly to the original frame.

The NormalizedSegment class

Turns the segment into data that is ready for fitting:

  • vertices: vertices in the normalized frame (points on the surface)
  • vertex_weights: an area weight for each vertex (positive, sums to 1)
  • facet_centroids: triangle centres in the normalized frame
  • facet_normals: the unit normal of each triangle
  • facet_weights: an area weight for each triangle (positive, sums to 1)

Why both vertices and triangles? Vertices serve the positional guesses (circle fits, angle computation). Triangle normals check whether the primitive type was guessed correctly (a plane's normal is constant; a cylinder's normal is perpendicular to its axis).

The prepare_segment(record) function

Computes the normalization:

  • center: the area-weighted centroid of all the triangles
  • scale: the area-weighted root mean square (RMS) distance of the triangle corners from the centroid

💡 Concept: centroid

The centre of mass of a shape. If every triangle of the segment weighs as much as its area, the centroid is the weighted average of the triangle centres:

centroid = Σ (areai × centeri) / Σ areai

This is better than a plain average of the vertices, because large triangles should rightly count for more.

💡 Concept: area weighting

When the triangles of a segment have very different sizes (adaptive triangulation, for example), weighting by area ensures that small triangles do not skew the guess or the fit.

Example: a cylinder segment with one large triangle at one end and many tiny triangles at the other. If every vertex counts the same, the dense region (with many vertices) has too much influence even though it covers little area.

2. primitives.py: distance and normal for each primitive

For each primitive type (plane, cylinder, cone, sphere, torus) there are two functions:

Distance functions

  • distance_to_plane(points, point, normal): distance from a point to a plane
  • distance_to_sphere(points, center, radius): distance from a point to a sphere
  • distance_to_cylinder(points, axis_point, axis_direction, radius)
  • distance_to_cone(points, apex, axis_direction, half_angle_radians)
  • distance_to_torus(points, center, axis_direction, major_radius, minor_radius)

Every distance is to the infinite surface (not bounded). This matters: a cone has no "top edge".

Normal functions

  • normal_of_*(points, ...): the unit surface normal at the nearest point

Why normals? As a check: if a segment really is a plane, the surface normal should equal (or be opposite to) the normals of the input triangles. A large angle between the two means the guess is off.

3. hypotheses.py: initial hypotheses

The central function: propose_hypotheses(record)

It returns a list of Hypothesis objects, one for every primitive type that can be computed.

Each hypothesis contains:

  • primitive_type: the primitive type
  • parameters: the parameters (in the input frame and units)
  • relative_rms: the relative residual (vertex-to-surface distance, in units of the normalization scale)
  • normal_rms_radians: the normal error (an angle in radians)

How each type is computed:

Type Method Data source
Plane PCA of the vertices → the weakest direction is the normal vertices
Sphere linear (algebraic) least squares vertices
Cylinder axis from PCA of the normals, radius from a circle fit normals and vertices
Cone axis and apex from the normals, angle from the vertices normals and vertices
Torus axis from Plücker coordinates, radii from a circle fit normals and vertices

Important: there are no iterations and no randomness. Every guess is a closed-form computation (SVD, least squares).

How the libraries contribute

NumPy

What: fast matrix computations
Used here for:

  • np.linalg.eigh: eigendecomposition of real symmetric matrices (PCA)
  • np.linalg.lstsq: linear least squares (sphere, circle fits, cone apex)
  • np.linalg.svd: the smallest singular vector (the torus axis in Plücker coordinates)
  • np.linalg.norm: vector lengths (normals, distances)
  • np.cross: cross products (normals, Plücker coordinates)
  • basic operations: matrix subtraction, dot products, broadcasting

Why? NumPy is precise on large arrays, and the same computation always gives the same result.

SciPy

What: advanced scientific algorithms
Used here for: nothing yet at this stage. The next stage (refinement with least squares) uses scipy.optimize.least_squares.

⚠ An interesting case: cylinder versus sphere

The problem: two rings of vertices (typical of a coarse cylinder) also lie on a sphere!

The solution: check the normals. On a cylinder the surface normal is perpendicular to the axis (angle error ~1.9°). On a sphere the surface normal points at the centre (angle error ~26.7°).

Type Vertex RMS Normal RMS
cylinder (correct) 1e-08 1.9°
sphere (wrong) 5e-09 26.7°

The vertex RMS is even smaller for the sphere, but the normals reveal the structure.

A change for the cone

For a cone frustum (mesh_03) the primitive is an infinite cone, not a truncated one. The facet normals come from chord planes, not tangent planes, so the angle they imply is slightly smaller than the true angle.

The fix: use the vertices to compute the angle exactly:

tan(half_angle) = (area_weighted (axial × radial)) / (area_weighted axial²)

This gives the angle exactly, independent of the frustum's height or size.

Next steps

Are the guesses good enough? Not always:

  • Torus: only ~1e-4 accuracy (the facet normals of a tessellated torus are tilted)
  • Cone: now 1e-8 accuracy, after switching to the vertices for the angle

The next step is refinement with SciPy: take the guess as a starting point and fit with SciPy's least_squares to get the best parameters under the L² norm of the distances.

Current limitations

  • CGAL not used: the closed-form guesses covered every real segment
  • Plotly optional: no visualization yet
  • No classification yet: only guesses, no final decision about the type
